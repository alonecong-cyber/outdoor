Best Rain Jackets of 2026

An in-depth look at the top rain jackets, from versatile hiking jackets to emergency shells

A great rain jacket should not only be reliably waterproof but also comfortable, lightweight, and packable (especially for hiking and backpacking), and feature the right features for your intended use. The Patagonia Torrentshell 3L has been our go-to for years, and it's joined us on adventures close to home in the Pacific Northwest as well as deep in the Patagonian wilderness. It boasts a very reasonable price for the performance it offers, and it even crosses over well into casual use, making it a true do-it-all waterproof layer. Our list doesn't end there; we also include ultralight rain jackets, budget-friendly options, and a few casual designs for those who don't need the utmost in on-trail performance. If you need a burlier shell for mountaineering or backcountry skiing, check out our list of the best hardshell jackets.

Best Overall Rain Jacket: Men's Patagonia Torrentshell 3L & Women's Patagonia Torrentshell 3L ($189)

Category: Daily use/hiking
Weight: 14.1 oz. (men's), 12.4 oz. (women's)
Waterproofing: 3L H2No Performance Standard
What we like: 3-layer performance, quality materials, and good looks at an excellent price.
What we don’t: A bit crinkly and stiff; no chest pocket.

Patagonia shook up the rain jacket market with the release of its Torrentshell 3L a few years ago. The jacket’s hardshell-like 3-layer construction (hence the “3L” in the name) is highly protective and durable, and the interior’s soft-touch tricot lining boosts comfort while limiting the clammy feeling that’s typical of sub-$200 rain jackets. In testing, material quality has been a common theme: Everything from the thick-toothed main zipper to the pit zips and the three-way adjustable hood has a solid, reliable feel. Tack on sleek Patagonia styling, a wide variety of available colors, and a price that undercuts the competition by hundreds, and the net result is one of the most well-rounded rain jackets we've tested to date.

One notable downside to the Torrentshell’s durable construction is that it’s stiffer and crinklier than many competitors (it does break in and loosen up a bit over time). Additionally, it doesn't have a chest pocket, and the shell isn’t a leader in terms of weight or packed size. In the end, if you want a high-performance piece for serious outdoor use, you may want to up your budget for an alternative like the Arc’teryx Beta SL. But we think the vast majority of folks will find the Torrentshell to be a near-perfect match for everyday and light backcountry use. For more information, see our in-depth review of the Patagonia Torrentshell 3L.

Best Budget Rain Jacket: Men's REI Co-op Rainier & Women's REI Co-op Rainier ($100)

Category: Daily use/hiking
Weight: 11.3 oz. (men's), 9.9 oz. (women's)
Waterproofing: 2.5L HydroWall
What we like: Great price for a solid all-around design; wide range of colors and sizes.
What we don’t: Fits very big, so we ended up sizing down.

REI’s in-house line of rain shells continues to impress us when it comes to bang for your buck, and the popular Rainier is no exception. Overall, we think they’ve put together a solid product: The in-house 2.5-layer construction holds up well in all-day drizzle, pit zips help keep you cool on the go, and the clean styling wears well just about anywhere. REI also improved comfort with a softer, quieter face fabric on the latest model, without increasing the cost, which we appreciate. Add REI’s excellent warranty and a wide range of colorways and sizes, and you get a quality, budget-friendly rain shell for everything from city commutes to summer hiking and backpacking trips.

In testing the Rainier, we were struck by how premium it felt, despite its price. Don't get us wrong, it's far from the sleek, high-performance (and pricey) designs in our list, but it doesn't feel cheap, either. The hood design is well-executed, and we appreciate the pockets and the fine details, such as cuff adjustability. That said, the Rainier isn't a performance piece. It's underbuilt for tackling heavy or sustained precipitation. It also fits large, and depending on how you plan to layer underneath, it may be best to go down a full size. But if you're mostly hiking in the warmer months or live in dry areas like Colorado and California, the Rainier is more than enough. And, best of all, it won't break the bank.

Best Performance Rain Jacket: Men's Arc’teryx Beta SL & Women's Arc’teryx Beta SL ($500)

Category: Performance/hiking
Weight: 12 oz. (men's), 10.6 oz. (women's)
Waterproofing: 3L Gore-Tex ePE
What we like: Strong combination of weight savings, protection, and performance-oriented features.
What we don’t: The priciest rain jacket on our list.

Arc’teryx is known for top-of-the-line quality and prices to match, and the British Columbia-based brand has long been our go-to for premium weather protection. Combining 3-layer Gore-Tex construction with a host of technical features (including a helmet-compatible hood and pit zips), the Beta SL blurs the line between rain jacket and hardshell and delivers trustworthy performance for 4-season hiking and backcountry exploring. And as we’ve come to expect from the brand, all of the Beta SL's details are nicely sorted, including an adjustable and helmet-compatible hood, Velcro-equipped cuffs, dual drawcords at the hem, water-resistant zippers, and Gore's C-Knit backer that adds a nice dose of next-to-skin comfort.

What’s not to like with the Beta SL? Most significant is the price: It’s the most expensive rain jacket on our list and out of reach for all but the most committed of outdoor adventurers. If you don't mind a few extra ounces and don't need the utmost in technical features, you can save some cash with the standard Beta Jacket, which is just as weather-proof. The Beta SL is also low on storage, with just three zippered pockets, and the "fitted" cut is streamlined, which is great for performance use but may mean some want to size up. We've had better luck with the generous cut of the Beta SV, which is the most durable (read: thick) and protective jacket of the bunch, and only really necessary for ice climbers and dedicated mountaineers. But overall, if you’re looking for versatile rain and snow protection without springing for a full-on hardshell, the Beta SL is hard to beat. For more details, see our in-depth review of the Arc'teryx Beta SL.

Best Stretchy Rain Jacket: Men's Black Diamond Fineline Stretch & Women's Fineline Stretch ($189)

Category: Hiking/daily use
Weight: 11.3 oz. (men's), 9.5 oz. (women's)
Waterproofing: 2.5L BD.dry
What we like: Stretchy, loaded with features, lightweight, and affordable.
What we don’t: The larger fit doesn’t match its performance intent; not the most breathable design.

Black Diamond took the plunge into the rain shell market with their popular Stormline, which was recently rebranded as the Fineline Stretch. The big news is the stretchy 2.5-layer construction, which offers greater comfort than a standard rain jacket. The interior is soft to the touch and doesn’t have the plasticky feel that you get with most traditional designs, and there’s a noticeable amount of “give” in the shell fabric. The jacket is also well-equipped and competitively lightweight, with two hand pockets, a coated front zipper, and an all-in weight of 11.3 ounces (9.5 oz. for the women's). To top it off, the Fineline is competitively priced.

We recently rocked the Fineline on a rainy backpacking and climbing trip in Wyoming's Wind River Range and came away very impressed. Features like a helmet-compatible hood, stuff pocket, and pit zips give the Fineline a clear performance slant, but we found the large fit to be less than ideal for the backcountry. It’s easy to layer a puffy underneath, but the jacket felt too long and roomy for us when wearing it over a thin baselayer (sizing down can help). We also found breathability to be lacking, leading to a clammy, sticky-feeling interior when working up a sweat (especially noticeable when stuffing wet hands into the pockets). But it’s hard to beat the Fineline's combination of weight, comfort, and price, earning it our vote as the best stretchy design this season. Best Ultralight Backpacking Rain Jacket: Enlightened Equipment Visp ($250)

Category: Hiking
Weight: 5 oz.
Waterproofing: 3L ripstop nylon w/ PU membrane
What we like: Incredibly lightweight but still nicely appointed; breathable yet waterproof; pit zips.
What we don’t: Availability can be hit-or-miss; the thin shell tears easily.

Once a little-known garage-grown company from Minnesota, Enlightened Equipment has exploded on the backpacking scene in recent years, consistently churning out some of the best ultralight gear. Their quilts, synthetic jackets, and even rain jackets are now seemingly ubiquitous in thru-hikers' and weekend warriors' packs. For those looking to shave weight without compromising on performance, look no further. The Visp was our author's rain jacket of choice for a 5,000-mile thru-hike of the Continental Divide Trail and Appalachian Trail, keeping him comfortable and dry the entire time. It began to wear out quicker near the end of his journey (and was decorated by Tenacious Tape), but lasted much longer than he anticipated, given the eight months of consistent torture it was put through. 

At 5 ounces, the Visp handily undercuts every jacket on this list without skimping on too many features. Pit zips and a full front zipper facilitate ventilation, Velcro cuffs and an adjustable hem and hood combat drafts, and a stiff wire brim in the hood keeps rain off your face. That said, the Visp fabric is highly sought after, and EE has struggled to keep it in stock over the years (it's out of stock at the time of publication). And the low weight does come with some other sacrifices: No hand (or chest) pockets mean there is no storage to speak of, and the 7-denier (D) shell—while stalwart for how thin it is—is fragile compared to most alternatives here. Our Visp survived several thru-hikes, but it felt like handling fine china as we delicately bushwhacked through overgrown sections and scrambled across sharp scree fields. That said, if ultralight minimalism is your modus operandi, the Visp is a special UL piece.

It's far from our first choice for performance use, but it can keep up on shorter day hikes just fine. But so long as you keep your expectations reasonable, the Downpour is an excellent choice and a true upgrade over most cheap rain jackets.

What we don’t: Hood could use a couple of improvements; heavier than past versions.

Outdoor Research's AscentShell membrane is a favorite among backcountry skiers and hikers, and stands out in the brand's Foray and Aspire rain jackets. Compared to traditional waterproof membranes, the AscentShell is stretchy and breathable while still providing wet-weather protection. Plus, with a thick 40D nylon shell, it's tough and able to withstand sustained moisture. You also get OR's TorsoFlo venting system, which features a generous side zipper that runs from the hem to the bicep, letting you dump heat fast or access a hipbelt or a pant pocket without taking the jacket off. At the end of the day, the Foray and Aspire are very comfortable yet protective shells, priced just right.

However, we do have a few nitpicks with the latest OR Foray 3L Jacket. Specifically, the hood drawcords can be finicky to adjust on the fly, and we’d like to see a stiffer brim for better protection in heavy wind or rain. The jacket also doesn't stuff down into its own pocket like some competitors, and it's not exactly a featherweight at just under a pound. Still, the Foray and Aspire's solid protection and smart venting make them among the most functional and adventure-friendly rain jackets you can find, and they're a great choice for those who need all-weather performance when venturing into technical terrain.

Extremely Light and Packable: Men's Rab Phantom Jacket & Women's Rab Phantom Jacket ($225)

Category: Running/hiking
Weight: 4.1 oz. (men's), 3.7 oz. (women's)
​Waterproofing: 2.5L Pertex Shield
What we like: Absurdly light and packable with an impressive wet-out time.
What we don’t: Slim fit; tight hood; no pockets or pit zips; finicky zipper.

This waterproof jacket seems to defy physics, dipping under five ounces while still offering dependable foul-weather protection in the mountains. Weighing a scant 4.1 ounces (3.7 oz. for the women's version), the Phantom soundly wins as the most packable waterproof jacket we've tested. This thing crams down to the size of a baseball (the roll-away hood doubles as an integrated storage pocket), yet it boasts a competitive wet-out time. We recently tested the latest iteration of the Phantom on an 850-mile bikepacking tour through Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan, and it fended off some heinous weather despite its light weight. We came away impressed by its capabilities in a wide variety of gnarly conditions.

So why didn't the Phantom snag our "best ultralight" award? In the end, its weight and packed size are about all it has going for it. To achieve such tiny specs, Rab cut some serious corners, and there are very few features to speak of. There are no pockets, pit zips, or cinch cords, and the fit is very slim. This limits layering underneath, ventilation, overall adjustability, and draft protection—all serious considerations in the backcountry. The zipper frequently snags on the protective zip guard, and we almost ripped the fabric several times on our bikepacking trip. If you want a more substantial rain jacket that still keeps things light, the Rab Phantom Mountain Jacket weighs 7.8 ounces, features a thicker fabric, and has a few more features like hand pockets and an adjustable hood. That said, if weight and space are of the utmost concern, the regular Phantom is tough to beat. And the 7D fabric feels much more durable than other similarly thin shells we've worn, and, despite our close calls, we crossed the finish line of our bike tour with zero rips or tears. A true testament to this jacket's longevity.

What Rain Protection Should I Wear While Moving?

Your layer system depends on the weather, of course, but it all begins with your next-to-skin layer. We most often recommend starting with a long-sleeve hiking shirt or sun hoodie over a short-sleeve shirt. Though it sounds counterintuitive, a long-sleeve shirt provides more protection in hot conditions because it provides a barrier between your skin and the sun. At some point, you will likely be hiking in your next-to-skin layer, even in cold weather. This layer should be breathable, moisture-wicking, and comfortable (merino or wool blends are our go-to, but synthetic shirts dry out faster). If you're hitting the trail in cool, morning conditions, a light windbreaker is a great active layer to go over a shirt. It provides light protection against wind and rain and traps some heat without becoming clammy. In very cold weather, a light synthetic jacket can be worn, but it often becomes too warm too quickly. And unless we're hiking in steady rain, we usually don't wear rain jackets, as their waterproof membranes can make them clammy and hot. A windbreaker won't provide total waterproofing, but it can cut the chill during infrequent rainstorms and dry out quickly once the storm has passed. In most cases, rain jackets and insulated layers are used as emergency layers and spend most of their lives stuffed in a backpack (both are handy around camp for extra warmth, though).
 

​Tested & Dismissed Rain Jackets

In this update, we dismissed the Marmot Minimalist Pertex rain jacket. For the same price, the Patagonia Torrentshell offers better performance and a sleeker fit. It's not a bad rain jacket, but we think there are better ones out there for the price. Check out our in-depth review of the Marmot Minimalist Pertex for more information.

We also removed the Montbell Versatile. Again, we had great experiences testing this jacket, but it's edged out by options like the Rab Phantom and Enlightened Equipment Visp. Both are less expensive and weigh less, making them better options for dedicated ULers.

The Black Diamond Highline Stretch also didn't make the cut this time. It's more expensive than the Arc'teryx Beta SL, and doesn't have the same level of waterproofing. We also prefer Arc'teryx's build and material quality, though it is stretchier and less crinkly than the Beta SL. For more details, read our in-depth review of the Black Diamond Highline Stretch.

We replaced the Marmot Precip Eco with the Rab Downpour in this update. Both are great upgrades over true budget rain jackets, but the Downpour edges out the PrecipEco on material quality and reliability in wet weather. For just $10 more, we think the Downpour is the better choice.

Lastly, we removed the Columbia Watertight II and women's Arcadia II. The REI Co-op Rainier costs the same, but has a better material quality, is more breathable, and is more performance-oriented with pit zips and better adjustability.

Our Rain Jacket Testing Process

Our rabble of rugged adventurers at Switchback Travel is no stranger to long days in the mountains blasted by incessant rain. From soaking-wet forests in the Pacific Northwest to driving storms on lofty peaks in Patagonia, we've taken our rain jackets on some wild adventures and allowed only the best to wiggle their way into this round-up. The initial list of 10 worthy jackets was created in 2015. Senior editor Chris Carter took over the guide in 2024. A seasoned thru-hiker and rock climber, Chris is intimately familiar with the importance of a dependable rain shell for safety and comfort in the backcountry. The guide is now managed by senior editor Maddie Downie. Maddie lives in one of the rainiest parts of the continental US (Bellingham, Washington) and practically lives in rain jackets through a good chunk of the year. But she doesn't let the rain keep her from hiking, backpacking, and trail running.

Our current list of 11 rain jackets represents the crème de la crème of these hardy shells. To test each model's mettle, we take them on rigorous, real-world adventures, gauging their ability to keep us dry and protected in wet conditions. We focus on key features like durability, packability, and breathability to help decide which jackets are best suited for technical performance use or more casual, daily wear. We know that not every outdoor mission is the same, which is why we included a wide variety. As the competition evolves, we’ll keep our finger on the pulse of the market, testing new models and technologies to keep the list above up to date.

Rain Jacket Categories

Daily Use
If you live in a wet climate like the Pacific Northwest or New England, a rain jacket is an indispensable part of your everyday wardrobe. For daily use, like commuting, running errands, or walking the dog, you’ll likely prioritize a casual fit (which accommodates a wide variety of layers underneath) and useful features like handwarmer pockets and Velcro wrist cinches. You won’t often see add-ons like helmet-compatible hoods or exposed, water-resistant zippers in this category, and daily use jackets don’t prioritize weight savings or packability (in fact, they’re often thick and durable). As a result, daily-use jackets are either budget-oriented or designed with style in mind, and some of our favorites include the Patagonia Torrentshell 3L (which overlaps nicely with the hiking category below) and the Rab Downpour. 

Hiking
Hiking-focused shells vie with those in our daily use category for the most common type of lightweight rain jacket. This designation includes big sellers like the entry-level Rab Downpour, as well as the expensive Arc’teryx Beta SL. Ultralight jackets like the Rab Phantom and Enlightened Equipment Visp work great as dedicated hiking shells as well. Hiking jackets are built to be light and packable for backcountry trips, and their performance in heavy rain and wind is admirable. Breathability does suffer compared with the performance shells below, and the cheaper jackets won't last as long, but hiking shells are a nice middle ground of price, weight, and protection.
 

Performance
Performance-oriented rain jackets are your focused outdoor pieces. One step down from a full-on hardshell jacket, they're made to withstand extreme conditions: The shell fabrics are tougher, the waterproof membranes are better breathers, and the hoods are more substantial. As a result, the interior fabrics are also much less prone to becoming clammy and often have a premium, soft feel. The price does increase with these upgrades, though daily usability is nearly on par with the hiking/daily use category above. The primary compromise is pocket placement, as some hand pockets sit high to accommodate a climbing harness or backpack hipbelt. Jackets like the Arc'teryx Beta SL and Patagonia M10 Storm Jacket fall into this category.
 

Waterproof vs. Water-Resistant

No piece of outdoor gear offers total protection from outside moisture, but most of today’s rain jackets are listed as being either “water-resistant” or “waterproof.” Many light rain jackets, windbreakers, and softshells are water-resistant, meaning that they shed water in light to moderate precipitation but aren’t completely waterproof under extended exposure. These jackets are often more breathable than their fully waterproof counterparts, but won't hold up to perpetual downpours. Instead, they shine on fast-and-light missions where efficiency, ventilation, and weight are of utmost concern. Waterproof jackets have a built-in laminate layer (like Gore-Tex) or a coating that essentially blocks outside moisture from entering under most conditions. Additionally, they have waterproof taping along the seams on the interior of the jacket.

You may notice a waterproof rating listed on some outdoor gear websites, represented by a number from 0 to 20,000mm or more. This is the amount of water in a 1-inch-diameter vertical tube that the material can withstand without leaking. The test is a strange one: It doesn’t mimic real-world conditions, and many manufacturers choose not to list it at all. Other factors, like seam taping, play a major role in waterproofness, so the number doesn’t truly determine how dry you will stay in a downpour. We don’t find waterproof ratings very helpful in the buying process and have chosen not to list them with our specs. Take note of the number when it’s available, as it often correlates with other characteristics like fabric thickness and durability, but don’t base your buying decision on that alone.
 

Fabric Layers

Creating a waterproof rain jacket that resists moisture while allowing sweat and hot air to vent requires a combination of fabric layers. You’ll see this referenced in every performance-oriented rain jacket, typically seen as: 2L, 2.5L, or 3L. We break down the pros and cons of each below, and for a deep dive, check out our article on rain jacket construction.

2-Layer
These jackets are the most basic and typically require a mesh liner to protect the jacket’s inner coating (hence the 2-layer name). They’re not very breathable, and the mesh adds bulk, making 2-layer jackets best for casual use. You’ll often find them in entry-level styles, such as the Columbia Watertight II (not included in our list). In the end, these pieces are ideal for everyday and frontcountry use, but their weight, bulk, and lack of breathability make them impractical for activities like hiking and backpacking.

2.5-Layer
A 2.5-layer jacket attaches a very thin interior fabric to the waterproof/breathable laminate or coating. The benefit of this interior finish is that the mesh found in 2-layer jackets is no longer necessary. Breathability and compressibility increase, and weight decreases with the design, making this the most popular option for hikers, backpackers, and climbers. One downside is that the interior fabric isn't as soft to the touch as a true 3-layer (some consider it slippery or plasticky), but we've seen improvements with recent models.
 

3-Layer
A true 3-layer construction incorporates three separate pieces of fabric, with the actual waterproof and breathable membrane in the middle and a more substantial fabric on the interior. This adds bulk over a comparable 2.5-layer, but increases durability and further improves moisture wicking and next-to-skin feel. Jumping to a 3-layer jacket also involves a significant price increase (one exception is Patagonia's Torrentshell 3L), and most often these are big-name designs, like Gore-Tex or eVent. It's worth noting that nearly all the premium performance jackets on our list have a 3-layer construction.
 

Durable Water-Repellent (DWR) Finish

In addition to the waterproof membrane, another key piece of a rain jacket’s protection is its durable water-repellent finish (commonly referred to as DWR). This coating is applied to the exterior of most rain jackets to prevent moisture from absorbing into the face fabric by beading up the droplets. A fresh DWR is impressive and can offer excellent protection in light to moderate conditions, though heavy, sustained rainfall will eventually overwhelm the coating (that’s where the waterproof membrane comes into play). Over time, the DWR finish will wear down, although you can keep it fresh by staying on top of maintenance.

A final note regarding DWR is that there has been a recent push to move away from traditional coatings that use perfluorocarbons, chemicals linked to environmental and health issues. It’s still a developing technology, and key brands like Patagonia haven’t fully switched yet, but PFAS-free options are becoming more prevalent.
 

Weight

A quick look at our comparison table above reveals that rain jacket weights correlate closely with their intended use(s). On the lightweight end of the spectrum are hiking-ready shells like the Black Diamond Fineline, while more feature-rich, durable designs that cross over into daily wear often add weight (such as the Cotopaxi Cielo). For those who want a well-rounded option, we’ve found the 9- to 16-ounce range is typically the sweet spot, though you can go as low as 6 ounces in the ultralight market (the Rab Phantom Jacket is the lightest option on our list at a scant 4.1 oz.).
 

Breathability

One of the most sought-after features in a waterproof rain jacket is breathability: the ability for perspiration and other moisture to exit the jacket without outside water coming in. Some cheaper rain jackets are barely breathable at all, but almost all of the fabrics used in today’s models are at least somewhat breathable and promoted as such. The market leader has long been Gore-Tex, particularly in their high-end "Active" and "Pro" offerings, but a number of fabrics are now challenging the paradigm, including AscentShell featured in the Outdoor Research Foray. Generally speaking, the more you spend, the more breathable the jacket will be. One exception is ultralight jackets, which cost more than cheap lightweight models but ventilate approximately the same in most cases. A jacket’s ability to keep you cool is greatly enhanced with the inclusion of pit zips, which we discuss further below.
 

Packability

A jacket’s packability can be looked at and measured in a couple of ways. First, there are the jackets that can be stuffed into their own pockets. Just turn the pocket inside out, smoosh the jacket in, and zip it shut. While this is great, it doesn’t necessarily mean the jacket is that packable. The packed sizes can vary widely for these “packable” shells, with the ultralights resembling a small envelope and others measuring about 3x the size. Another way to look at packable rain shells is how compressible they are. In that respect, the lightweight Arc'teryx Beta SL would still be considered “packable" despite lacking a stuff pocket. You can just roll it up into its own hood to protect the thinner fabric in your pack. Look to weight as a great indicator of how packable a jacket is. The most packable jacket on our list is the Rab Phantom Jacket, which crams down to about the size of a baseball and weighs a minuscule 4.1 ounces.
 

Features

Rain jackets don’t offer as much variation in features as some other types of outdoor gear, but there are notable differences between models. Many ultralight and trail-running jackets forego pockets to cut weight, while others sport them in abundance. Some rain jackets offer pit zips and/or full side vents, while basic models do not (and more expensive models often feature taped seams for extra protection from the elements). Almost all rain jackets include hoods, but some are cut large enough to fit over a bike or climbing helmet, and the cinch style varies significantly. Keep a close eye on features and try to match them to your intended use and budget.

Pockets
Casual users appreciate a couple of hand pockets, and that’s one of the most notable omissions in ultralight shells for daily use. Most ultralight shells go without hand pockets, instead opting for a chest pocket for storage. Some jackets, like Enlightened Equipment's Visp, forego pockets entirely to shave weight. And the Patagonia Storm Racer just has one chest pocket. On the other hand, more feature-rich shells, such as our hiking and daily-use options, strike a better sweet spot for everyday users. You often get two hand pockets and a chest pocket (either on the inside or outside of the shell), all the better for the little things you need to carry daily, like a phone or wallet.

Pocket placement is another consideration. Serious shells, such as those offered by Arc’teryx, often place the hand pockets higher up on the torso to avoid interfering with your pack’s hipbelt (they're often referred to as "hipbelt- or harness-compatible). What you gain in convenience with the hipbelt (or climbing harness), you then lose in daily usability. It’s just not as natural a landing spot for your hands. As such, if you plan to wear your rain shell the majority of the time around town, it's best to select a rain jacket with hand pockets at the standard height.
 

Hood
Hood size is a major consideration when shopping for a rain jacket. If you plan to climb in your rain jacket, look for one with a helmet-compatible hood. These can reach over the top of most climbing helmets for added weather protection. But for normal hiking and backpacking, it’s often prudent to avoid this feature, as the large hood requires a lot of cinching, which can cause the fabric to bunch.

The hood's adjustability is also key. When the wind is blowing, you want a hood that conforms to your head, while retaining enough structure around the sides and the bill that you can still see out. Some manufacturers succeed better than others at this concept. One standout is Arc’teryx’s StormHood (as seen on the Beta SL): With a single pull at the back of the hood, you adjust evenly around the sides and back of the head. We prefer the toggle style for adjusting the back of the hood over a rip-and-stick Velcro tab for its improved performance. The cord wraps around the sides of your head and cinches the fabric evenly, keeping the hood in place even in windy conditions. The benefits of the Velcro style are its simplicity and weight: It doesn’t require a cord or toggle, both of which add bulk.
 

Pit Zips, Side Zips, and Core Vents
When creating a waterproof jacket designed to keep moisture out, there are natural limits on how much air it can hold. And when you’re working hard, it can quickly become a necessity to dump some of that hot air rather quickly. Enter the pit zip. By opening the jacket under the arms, you can release a lot of air without compromising its waterproof design. The ultimate expression of a pit zip's effectiveness is the Outdoor Research Foray 3L Jacket. The underarm zippers extend all the way down to the hems on either side. Full unzipped, the jacket becomes a poncho.

Because adding pit zips to a jacket inevitably adds a slight weight, some manufacturers like REI, Rab, and Outdoor Research have come up with a creative solution: core vents. By lining some of their designs' handwarmer pockets with airy mesh, they double as vents when unzipped. It’s not perfect, however (opening your pockets to vent means that anything stored inside could fall out, and it doesn’t dump heat as quickly as the traditional pit zip), but it does save weight while offering a boost in ventilation.
 

Waist Adjustments
To achieve the best seal possible, every rain jacket we recommend here has a cinch system at the hem. Typically done with a cord and toggle, they’re very user-friendly. You’ll see one side cinch on ultralights when the manufacturer is trying to cut some weight. The single cinch does mean that if you really have to tighten the jacket, it will pull to one side, but it’s often negligible and worth the weight savings. Heavier rain jackets have cinches on each side for a more even fit.
 

Rain Jackets with Built-In Stretch

Over the past few years, the number of waterproof rain jackets featuring built-in stretch has grown. Designs like Black Diamond’s Fineline incorporate fabrics and a waterproof internal membrane that flex easily. For performance use, the benefits are obvious: While climbing, hiking, or doing other activities where you’re moving your arms a lot, a stretchy jacket is less restrictive. But we’ve also enjoyed the greater level of comfort and less crinkly feel for daily wear. In general, a stretchy rain jacket will cost more than a standard shell, but it’s a nice upgrade that comes with plenty of tangible benefits.
 

Rain Jacket FAQ

What's the difference between rain jackets, softshells, and hardshells?

Unlike hardshells and rain jackets, traditional softshell jackets are not waterproof. The soft, flexible outer fabric is typically reinforced with a DWR coating, but the seams aren't taped, and the fabric will eventually let water seep through. Softshells are also thicker, warmer, and heavier than rain jackets. Even as technologies have advanced and full-weatherproof softshells have become available, they still can’t match the waterproofing performance of a traditional rain jacket. Instead, softshells remain a better choice for those seeking a breathable, water-resistant outer layer. Popular applications include backcountry skiing and trekking in mild weather.

Hardshell jackets, on the other hand, are made for extreme conditions. Built to withstand heavy driving rain and wind, the jackets are heavier and bulkier. Their fabrics are also much less prone to being soaked through under sustained rainfall. As a result of the tough builds, you’ll see these hardshells being used for anything from mountaineering to backcountry skiing. And you’ll also see prices skyrocket for these performance pieces thanks to their high-end detailing. A few rain jackets on this list cross into the lighter end of the hardshell category, including the Arc'teryx Beta SL.
 

How do I wash and maintain the waterproofing on my rain jacket?

A rain jacket’s waterproofing relies on a combination of factors, including a DWR coating and clean fabric layers on either side of the waterproof and breathable membrane to allow air vapor to pass through. Some membrane designs are more prone to clogging and require consistent cleaning (this will vary based on use). For washing, it’s always best to start by checking the label on your jacket, as the specific instructions will vary. As a general recommendation, the following works well for us: Wash the jacket in warm water with liquid detergent (Nikwax Tech Wash is our go-to), then run it through a second rinse cycle to remove any detergent residue. Line drying is typically best, although we’ve had some eVent and Gore-Tex jackets that instruct you to put them in the dryer on warm heat to revive the DWR finish.

If you notice that your jacket isn’t beading up water anymore and putting it in the dryer for a short stretch doesn’t fix the problem, you may need to reapply the DWR coating (this is common as the finish diminishes over time). A waterproof jacket without DWR won’t breathe as well in heavy rain because water will pool and soak into the exterior fabric. Reapplying the DWR is a simple process, and we’ve found that the Nikwax TX.Direct Spray-On works well (just follow the instructions on the bottle).
 

What do I give up in going with a budget rain jacket?

A budget rain jacket can be a perfectly fine choice for casual or infrequent use, but they generally aren't the most reliable in performance settings. When it comes to performance, budget rain jackets are more prone to wetting out than expensive rain jackets, due to their less premium waterproof membranes. Cheap rain jackets are usually 2-layer (rather than 2.5 or 3-layer), which means they're heavier, bulkier, and less breathable. Durability can also be a concern, but we've had decent luck with thick shells on budget jackets (ironically, some expensive jackets can use thin materials to cut weight, making them more prone to wear and tear). Technical features will also be low (such as harness-compatible pockets and underarm vents), but most budget jackets have plenty of storage and generous cuts that accommodate thicker layers underneath. But if you don't need the utmost in performance, a budget rain jacket can get the job done.
 

How should a rain jacket fit? Should I size up to layer a fleece underneath?

In our experience, most casual rain jackets have a more generous fit than performance rain jackets. The trim fit of performance rain jackets is great for keeping things streamlined, but it can make it tough to layer thicker layers underneath. It's not uncommon for us to size up on a rain jacket so it comfortably fits a down or synthetic jacket under it. When possible, we recommend trying on a rain jacket in person, as it's the best way to determine the best fit. Bring a thicker layer to wear underneath, and when you have the rain jacket on in the store, move your arms around and walk around to ensure the fit is unrestricted. If shopping in person isn't an option, most manufacturer sites offer a size chart to help narrow down a proper fit. Taking a glance at customer reviews can usually give you an idea of how it fits. You can also purchase two or more sizes with the intent of returning the ones that don't fit, but you'll want to check the return policy before doing so.
